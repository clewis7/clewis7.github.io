Introduction

Proteins and folding

A protein is a chain. Twenty different building blocks called amino acids, strung together in an order written in your DNA, typically a few hundred long. Each link in the chain is a residue, numbered from one end to the other.

But the chain doesn't stay a chain. It collapses into a specific, reproducible three-dimensional shape, called a fold, and that shape is what the protein actually is. The shape creates the pocket that binds a drug, positions the reactive atoms that catalyze a reaction, forms the surface that sticks to another protein. Change the shape and you change the function — a single substitution in the wrong position can destroy it entirely.

Protein sequences can be read very efficiently using high-throughput DNA sequencing, which is why sequence databases hold hundreds of millions of entries. Structures come from X-ray crystallography, NMR, or cryo-electron microscopy, each of which has historically taken months of work per protein. After fifty years, the Protein Data Bank holds only a few hundred thousand.

AlphaFold2

AlphaFold 21 predicts protein structure from the underlying sequence.

The core insight of the model isn't really about the sequence you hand it. It's about that sequence's relatives.

The model first searches enormous databases for homologs of the input protein, the same protein in other species diverged over evolutionary time, and stacks them into an alignment. That alignment carries hidden three-dimensional information: if two residues touch each other in the folded structure, a mutation at one tends to be compensated by a mutation at the other, so their positions co-vary across evolutionary time.

AlphaFold turns those correlations into a running hypothesis about the relationship between every pair of residues in the chain — a large matrix it refines through 48 successive blocks, enforcing geometric consistency along the way. Only at the very end does it convert that hypothesis into actual atomic coordinates: the predicted fold.

Inside AlphaFold2

The structure at the top of this page is the last frame of a computation. Before AlphaFold produced those coordinates, it spent 48 layers refining an internal object you never see: a matrix holding its running belief about the relationship between every pair of residues in the chain.

Appendix

A1

Color Modes

  • Confidence (pLDDT) — AlphaFold reports how much it trusts each residue, 0 to 100, and this is the coloring the AlphaFold Database uses. Dark blue is very high confidence, light blue confident, yellow low, orange very low. Low-confidence regions are worth paying attention to: they often aren't modeling failures at all, but parts of the protein that genuinely have no fixed shape.
  • Secondary structure — the local patterns the backbone falls into. Helices in red, strands in yellow, everything else in grey. This is a property of the fold rather than of the prediction, so it's what you'd see coloring an experimentally determined structure too.
  • Sequence position — blue at the start of the chain running to red at the end. Useful for following the path the chain takes through the fold, which is otherwise hard to trace once it's folded up.
